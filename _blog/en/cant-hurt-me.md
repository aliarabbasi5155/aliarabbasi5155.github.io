---
title: "Book Review: \"Can't Hurt Me\" by David Goggins - Stop Lying to Yourself"
date: 2026-09-30 18:00
topic: Book Review
summary: Next to the quiet, introspective books I've written about here, "Can't Hurt Me" feels like someone kicking the door open and yelling. It's loud, extreme, and sometimes too much. It's also the most direct book I've read about the lies we tell ourselves...
---

After a run of books about guilt, death, freedom, and meaning, David Goggins' "Can't Hurt Me" was a very different experience. Goggins doesn't want to discuss your existential concerns. He wants you to get up and go for a run. And strangely, after all that philosophy, it was refreshing to hear.

His story is hard to believe even when you know it's true. He grew up with a violently abusive father, then dealt with poverty, racism, and learning difficulties he hid by cheating his way through school. In his twenties he weighed nearly 300 pounds and was spraying restaurants for cockroaches on night shifts, going nowhere. Then he watched a documentary about Navy SEALs, lost more than 100 pounds in under three months to qualify, and went through three Hell Weeks before he finally made it. After that came Army Ranger School, ultramarathons, and a pull-up world record, much of it, as he later found out, with a hole in his heart.

But the book isn't really about the achievements. The core message is simple: most of us operate far below our capacity, and what holds us back usually isn't our body or our circumstances but the stories we tell ourselves. That's where it connected, for me, with "The Fall." Clamence is a master at telling himself flattering stories and then talking about them beautifully. Goggins spends the entire book tearing his own stories apart, and he isn't gentle about it.

A few of his tools are genuinely practical, and these are the ones that stuck with me:

**The Accountability Mirror.** Write your goals, insecurities, and excuses on sticky notes, put them on your bathroom mirror, and face them every day. No flattering version, no audience, just you and the truth about where you actually are.

**The Cookie Jar.** Keep a list, mental or real, of hard things you've already survived and accomplished, and reach into it when you hit a wall. It isn't nostalgia. It's evidence that you're capable of more than your current mood is telling you.

**The 40% Rule.** When your mind says you're done, you're probably only about 40% done. It obviously isn't literally true every time, but as a reminder that the first "I can't" is usually a negotiation rather than a fact, it's surprisingly useful. I notice it most during deep work: the moment I want to switch to something easier is often right before the real progress starts.

That said, I don't agree with everything, and I don't think the goal is to copy his life. At some point his relationship with pain stops looking like strength and starts looking like something he can't switch off: running on fractured legs, pushing through injuries that clearly needed rest. Having just read Yalom, I couldn't help noticing how well parts of the story fit what Yalom calls the belief in "specialness," the need to prove over and over that normal limits don't apply to you. I don't say that to dismiss him; he is quite candid about what that intensity has cost him. But I think the healthiest way to read this book is to take the principle (stop lying to yourself and do the hard thing) and leave the extreme version to him.

In a way, "Can't Hurt Me" is "Mindset" with the volume turned all the way up. Dweck explains why the way you see yourself matters; Goggins shows what happens when someone decides, violently, to see himself differently. Each chapter also ends with a concrete challenge, which makes it hard to read passively. If you can, try the audiobook: Goggins and his co-author Adam Skolnick talk between the chapters, and it adds a lot.

Reading it didn't turn me into an ultramarathon runner. But it did make me notice how often I say "I'm tired" when what I really mean is "this is uncomfortable." That distinction alone was worth the book.
