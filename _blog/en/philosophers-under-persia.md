Say "Greek philosophy" and most of us picture Athens: Socrates arguing in the agora, Plato's Academy, white marble. But philosophy wasn't born in Athens. It was born on the coast of what is now western Turkey, in Ionia, and for most of the sixth and fifth centuries BC that coast belonged to the Achaemenid Persian Empire.

## When Ionia was Persian

Around 546 BC, Cyrus the Great defeated King Croesus of Lydia and took his capital, Sardis. Within a year or two his generals had brought the Greek cities of the coast under Persian rule. They were governed from Sardis, the seat of a satrap, usually through local Greek rulers loyal to the king. That lasted through the Ionian Revolt (499–493 BC), which Persia crushed, until the Persian defeat at Mycale in 479 BC. Across the Aegean, on the coast of Thrace, Persia ruled from about 512 to 479 BC.

So for roughly two generations, being an Ionian Greek meant being a subject of the King of Kings. And those were exactly the generations that invented philosophy.

## Who lived there, and where

### Heraclitus · Ephesus

*Today: the ruins near Selçuk, İzmir, Turkey.*

"Everything flows; you cannot step into the same river twice." Heraclitus was born around 535 BC and lived nearly his whole life under Persian rule. Diogenes Laertius even preserves a letter in which Darius the Great invites him to the Persian court, and Heraclitus' polite refusal. The letters are almost certainly later inventions, but someone found the story believable: of course the king would want the wisest man of his own province.

### Anaximenes · Miletus

*Today: near Balat, Didim, Aydın, Turkey.*

The third of the Milesian thinkers, who said that everything is air, thickened into water and stone or thinned into fire. Miletus struck a deal with Cyrus and kept the same terms it had had under Lydia, so its thinkers kept working while its ships traded across the empire. The last twenty years or so of Anaximenes' life were spent as a Persian subject.

### Anaxagoras · Clazomenae

*Today: near Urla, İzmir, Turkey.*

Born around 500 BC, a subject of the Persian king. He taught that the sun is a burning stone bigger than the Peloponnese, and that a cosmic Mind (*nous*) set everything in order. He moved to Athens around 480 BC, the year Xerxes invaded (one scholar has even suggested he arrived as a conscript in Xerxes' army), became a friend of Pericles, and was later put on trial there for impiety. Athens' first resident philosopher was an immigrant from the Persian Empire.

### Protagoras · Abdera

*Today: near Avdira, Xanthi, Greece.*

"Man is the measure of all things." Abdera was refounded around 545 BC by Greeks from Teos who were fleeing the Persians; about thirty years later, the Persians reached Thrace too, and the city was in their hands during Protagoras' childhood. Philostratus tells that his father hosted Xerxes on the march to Greece, and in thanks the king had his Magi teach the boy, which is supposedly why Protagoras later said he could not know whether the gods exist. It's a legend (the same story is told of his fellow citizen Democritus), but a telling one.

### Herodotus · Halicarnassus

*Today: Bodrum, Muğla, Turkey.*

Not a philosopher but the "father of history". He was born around 484 BC in a city ruled by Artemisia, the Carian queen who commanded ships for Xerxes at Salamis. And in his *Histories*, the earliest surviving Greek debate on whether democracy, oligarchy or monarchy is the best government is spoken by Persian nobles (Book 3, 80–82).

## A few careful footnotes

- **Thales** and **Anaximander** did their main work before the Persians arrived; Thales died around the time they came. He is famous for predicting a solar eclipse that stopped a battle between the Lydians and the Medes, an Iranian people, in 585 BC.
- **Pythagoras** left Samos before Persia took the island (around 517 BC). The story that he studied with the Magi in Babylon comes from a biography written some eight centuries later.
- **Democritus** was born in Abdera after the Persians had left. **Xenophanes** left Colophon when they arrived, and later wrote that at dinner people ask each other: "How old were you when the Mede came?"

None of this makes them Persian rather than Greek. They wrote and thought in Greek, and several of them fought against Persian rule. The point is simpler: the border we draw in our heads between "Greek" and "Persian" was not where these people lived. The first philosophers grew up on the empire's western edge, where Greek, Lydian, Carian and Persian worlds met, traded and argued.
